§ 3.4 · Opcode Execution
The
Stack
Machine

Bitcoin Script executes as a simple stack machine. Each opcode reads from the top of the stack, performs its operation, and pushes the result. No loops, no recursion — just a linear sequence of transformations.

This intentional simplicity makes Script auditable: any validator can trace execution step by step and verify the outcome deterministically.

Right-shift halving. Rather than dividing by 2 each halving epoch, Satoshi uses a bit-shift. At epoch 33 (year ~2140), the shift exceeds 64 bits and the subsidy rounds to zero — ending new issuance permanently.

About the Book

The Source Code Behind the Genesis Block

On January 9, 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto announced Bitcoin v0.01 to the Cryptography Mailing List. It was complete, functional software — written in C++ with a wxWidgets GUI, BerkeleyDB storage, and a peer-to-peer network built on raw TCP sockets. This book is a complete, line-by-line reading of that code.

Bitcoin v0.01 is 31,000 lines spread across 32 source files. In that space it implements proof-of-work mining, UTXO-based transactions, ECDSA digital signatures, a stack-based scripting language, P2P networking with IRC bootstrapping, wallet management, and a full desktop GUI. Everything Nakamoto needed to demonstrate the concept — written before any of it had a name.

"The root problem with conventional currency is all the trust that's required to make it work. The central bank must be trusted not to debase the currency, but the history of fiat currencies is full of breaches of that trust."

Satoshi Nakamoto · P2P Foundation · February 11, 2009

The annotations explain every significant function, data structure, and design choice — connecting code to its cryptographic foundations, to the history of digital cash that preceded it, and to the broader architectural decisions that made Bitcoin resilient enough to survive. Six primer chapters establish the necessary context: C++, cryptography, networking, and the cypherpunk movement.

This is an annotated edition in the tradition of The Annotated Turing and Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs — a close reading of source code as both a technical document and a historical artifact.
